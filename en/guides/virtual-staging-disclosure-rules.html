Virtual staging disclosure rules: EU AI Act, UK and US explained

Is virtual staging legal? Disclosure rules for listing photos under the EU AI Act, UK consumer law and US NAR/MLS rules, with sample label wording to copy.

Updated 4 October 2026 · staged.ai editorial team · 7 min read

Quick answer

Yes, virtual staging is legal in the EU, UK and US, but it must not mislead. From 2 August 2026 the EU AI Act requires a clear label on AI-generated or manipulated images that could pass as real. The UK and US have no AI-specific rule, but misleading property images breach consumer law, ASA rules and NAR ethics.

Virtual staging is legal in the European Union, the United Kingdom and the United States. What the law cares about is whether a buyer could be misled. Since 2 August 2026, the EU AI Act adds a specific duty: AI-generated or AI-manipulated images that could pass as real must be clearly labelled. The UK and US have no AI-specific labelling law for property, but misleading listing images already break consumer protection rules, advertising codes and, for US REALTORS, the NAR Code of Ethics. This guide, updated October 2026, sets out what each jurisdiction requires and gives you label wording you can copy.

Please note. This guide is general information, not legal advice. AI rules and guidance are changing quickly. For a specific listing or campaign, check with a lawyer, your professional body or your MLS.

Is virtual staging legal?

Yes, as long as it does not misrepresent the property. Showing buyers what an empty room could look like is accepted marketing practice. Making them believe the property already looks that way is not. European media have started calling the bad version "housefishing": AI-enhanced listing photos that make a home look more appealing than it is, for example by adding furniture or making rooms look larger (>Euronews, 25 September 2026).

What does the EU AI Act require for real estate photos?

Article 50 of the >AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689) sets transparency duties that apply from 2 August 2026. Two paragraphs matter for property marketing, and they apply to different people.

Who Duty Applies from
Deployer: the professional using the AI tool (agent, agency, photographer, host) Art. 50(4): disclose that a "deep fake" image has been artificially generated or manipulated 2 Aug 2026
Provider: the company that makes the generative AI system Art. 50(2): mark outputs in a machine-readable format so they are detectable as AI-generated 2 Aug 2026; extended to 2 Dec 2026 only for systems already on the market before 2 Aug 2026 (Digital Omnibus)

Sources: >Article 50 text; >Usercentrics; >Addleshaw Goddard.

The Act defines a deep fake (Art. 3(60)) as AI-generated or manipulated image, audio or video content that resembles existing persons, objects, places, entities or events and would falsely appear to a person to be authentic or truthful. A real flat is an existing place, and a photorealistic AI-furnished room can look authentic. The disclosure must be clear and distinguishable at the latest at the first exposure (Art. 50(5)), so a footnote buried at the end of a long description is not enough if the staged photo is your lead image.

Fines for breaching the transparency duties reach €15 million or 3% of worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher. A voluntary EU Code of Practice on transparency of AI-generated content was published on 10 June 2026 (>Usercentrics).

Is virtual staging really a "deep fake"?

That is genuinely debated. You could argue that a clearly illustrative staged room, in a market where staging is common, does not "falsely appear" authentic. Until regulators publish guidance or cases emerge, the practical answer is simple: always label, and keep the original photo next to the staged one. National consumer law, which in every member state bans misleading actions and omissions, applies either way.

What are the UK rules for AI images in property marketing?

There is no blanket legal requirement in the UK to disclose AI use in property adverts, but the ban on misleading marketing is stricter than a simple labelling rule. The unfair commercial practices provisions of the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024 took effect on 6 April 2025 and allow the CMA to fine businesses directly, up to 10% of global turnover. Estate agents' adverts are also covered by the CAP Code, enforced by the ASA (>Intellopro).

The test is whether the image gives a false impression of the property, not whether AI was used. The ASA has said that disclosure alone is very unlikely to fix a fundamentally misleading message. In other words, a label protects honest staging; it does not rescue an image that hides damp, widens a room or swaps the view. Material information guidance from National Trading Standards was withdrawn in 2025, with government guidance expected during 2026, so watch this space.

What are the US rules for virtual staging?

In the US, disclosure is driven by professional rules rather than a federal AI law.

  • NAR Code of Ethics, Article 12: "REALTORS® shall be honest and truthful in their real estate communications and shall present a true picture in their advertising, marketing, and other representations." Standard of Practice 12-10 also prohibits misleading consumers, "including use of misleading images" (>NAR).
  • MLS rules: many MLSs require virtually staged photos to be labelled, and some also require an unaltered photo of the same room. Rules vary by MLS, so check yours before you upload.
  • General advertising law: federal and state rules against deceptive advertising apply to property marketing like any other.

What can and can't you change in a listing photo?

The rule that works in all three jurisdictions: change furnishing and decor, never structure, condition, size or surroundings.

Edit Risk Practical guidance
Add furniture and decor to an empty room Low if labelled Label + show the original
Remove the seller's furniture Low if labelled Label "furniture digitally removed"
Show an unbuilt renovation (floors, walls) Medium Label as a "visualisation", never unlabelled
Correct light, colour, verticals Very low Label optional
Hide cracks, damp, mould, exposed pipes High Don't
Change the window view, enlarge rooms, move walls or windows High Don't
Remove permanent outside features (pylons, building sites) High Don't

Virtual staging disclosure checklist

  1. Label the image itself, not just the description. Corner placement, high contrast, legible at thumbnail size.
  2. Show the original right after the staged version in the gallery. The staged.ai report already pairs before and after.
  3. Mention it in the listing text, naming which photos are edited.
  4. Never use a staged image as the only photo of a room.
  5. Don't touch structure, defects, dimensions or views.
  6. Keep originals and edited files, dated, in case of a complaint.
  7. Tell the seller in writing that you will use virtual staging and how you will label it.
  8. Check your MLS or portal rules before publishing.
  9. Keep the label visible when you crop for social formats such as 9:16 stories.
  10. Say it out loud at viewings: "the furniture in the photos was virtual".

Sample virtual staging label wording

On the image (short): - "Virtually staged · AI-edited image" - "Virtually staged. Unstaged photo follows." - "Furniture digitally removed (AI)" - "AI renovation visualisation · works not carried out"

In the listing description: - "Some photos in this listing have been virtually staged using AI for illustration only. Furniture shown is not included in the sale. Unedited photos of each room are included in the gallery." - "Photos 3, 5 and 7 show a possible renovation visualised with AI. The works have not been carried out; any costs mentioned are estimates to be confirmed by contractor quotes."

In a client report or proposal: - "Images generated with AI for illustration. Cost estimates are indicative, based on average local market rates, and must be confirmed by a site visit and real quotes."

Does the same apply to decluttering and virtual renovation?

Broadly yes, with different intensity. Removing furniture and visualising a renovation change what the buyer sees, so always label them; see remove furniture from photos and virtual renovation. Light retouching of exposure or colour is closer to normal photo editing; Art. 50(2) even exempts assistive editing that does not substantially alter content from the provider marking duty. Frequently asked questions

Do I have to disclose virtual staging if no AI was used?

Under the EU AI Act, the labelling duty covers content generated or manipulated by AI systems. But consumer protection law in the EU, UK and US applies to any technique, including 3D renders and manual retouching. If a buyer could think the furniture is real or included in the sale, disclose it. The safest habit is to label every staged image, however it was made.

Who is responsible for labelling: the agent or the software company?

Under the EU AI Act, the duty to disclose deep fakes falls on the deployer, meaning the professional who uses the AI tool and publishes the image, usually the agency, agent or photographer. The software provider has a separate duty to mark outputs in a machine-readable way. Under consumer law, the business advertising the property is responsible for the listing as a whole.

Does basic photo editing like brightness or colour correction count?

Generally no. Adjusting exposure, white balance or straightening verticals does not change what the photo shows, so it is unlikely to be a deep fake or a misleading representation. Removing objects, replacing the sky, changing finishes or adding furniture is different: the image no longer shows the property as it is, so label it and keep the original available.

What are the penalties for misleading AI listing photos?

In the EU, breaching the AI Act transparency duties can lead to fines of up to 15 million euros or 3% of worldwide annual turnover. In the UK, the CMA can now fine businesses directly for unfair practices under the DMCC Act 2024. In the US, MLS rules can lead to fines or listing removal, and REALTORS face ethics complaints under Article 12.

Is virtual staging allowed on Zillow, Rightmove or Idealista?

Portals generally accept virtually staged images, but each sets its own listing rules, and in the US your MLS rules usually flow through to syndicated portals. Before publishing, check the current terms of the portal and your MLS, label the staged images and include at least one unedited photo of each staged room.
